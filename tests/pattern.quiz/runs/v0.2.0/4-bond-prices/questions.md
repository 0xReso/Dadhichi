How bond prices react to interest rates · medium · 60 questions
Assumptions: face value 100; coupons are paid once a year at year end; yields are annual rates with annual compounding; prices are taken just after a coupon has been paid; every promised payment is made (no default); a rate change happens instantly and then stays put unless the question says otherwise; "yield" means the bond's own yield to maturity, and where one rate is given for several bonds, all of them share it.

## Band 1: One rule at a time

**Q1.** A bond pays nothing until it repays 100 in exactly 2 years. The market yield for this bond is 5% a year. What is its price today?
(a) 90.00 (b) 90.70 (c) 90.91 (d) 100.00

**Q2.** You paid 100 for a bond that pays a coupon (a fixed yearly interest payment) of 4 for 3 years and then repays 100. The next day, the market yield on bonds like it rises from 4% to 6%. What happens to your bond's price?
(a) It rises above 100, because rates are higher
(b) It stays at 100, because it still repays 100
(c) It falls below 100
(d) It stays at 100, because its coupon rises to 6

**Q3.** You hold 100 in a savings account whose rate always follows the market rate, and a bond with face value 100 that you bought at 100 last week, paying a coupon of 4. Overnight, the savings rate and market bond yields both rise from 4% to 6%. How much interest does each pay you over the next year?

**Q4.** Three 5-year bonds all yield 6%. Bond A pays a coupon of 4, Bond B pays 6, Bond C pays 8. Rank their prices from highest to lowest, and say which, if any, is priced at exactly 100.

**Q5.** A 5-year bond pays a coupon of 6 and costs 120. Its current yield (coupon divided by price) is 6 / 120 = 5%. Its yield to maturity is the single annual rate that makes all its discounted future payments add up to today's price, which is the yearly return you lock in by buying now and holding to the end. Its yield to maturity is:
(a) exactly 5% (b) above 5% (c) below 5% (d) exactly 6%

**Q6.** A bond pays a coupon of 8, has 4 years left and yields 5%, so it costs 110.64. Its yield stays exactly 5% for the next year. One year from now, just after that year's coupon, its price is:
(a) 110.64 (b) above 110.64 (c) below 110.64 but above 100 (d) exactly 100

**Q7.** Bond A and Bond B both pay a coupon of 4 and both yield 4%, so both cost 100. A matures in 2 years; B matures in 20 years. Yields on both rise to 5%. Which loses a larger percentage of its price?
(a) Bond A (b) Bond B (c) Both lose the same, since both yields rose 1 point (d) Neither, since both still repay 100

**Q8.** Two 10-year bonds both yield 5%. Bond A pays a coupon of 2; Bond B pays 9. Both yields rise to 6%. Which pair of percentage losses is right?
(a) A loses 8.2%, B loses 6.7%
(b) A loses 6.7%, B loses 8.2%
(c) Both lose 7.4%
(d) Both lose 10%

**Q9.** Macaulay duration is the average time until a bond's payments arrive, where each payment's time is weighted by that payment's value today (its present value). A 3-year bond pays a coupon of 10 and yields 10%, so it costs 100. Its Macaulay duration is closest to:
(a) 3.00 years (b) 2.74 years (c) 2.00 years (d) 2.77 years

**Q10.** A perpetuity is a bond that pays its coupon every year forever and never repays. One pays 5 a year and yields 5%, so it costs 100. Its yield falls to 4%. Its new price is:
(a) 100 (b) 101 (c) 120 (d) 125

**Q11.** A floating-rate note (FRN) is a bond whose coupon is reset each year to that year's market rate. A 5-year FRN has just reached its reset date. At that moment the market rate jumps from 3% to 6%, and the FRN's coupon for the coming year is set at 6%. A 5-year fixed bond paying 3 falls from 100 to 87.36 on the same news. The FRN's price is now:
(a) 100 (b) about 87.36 (c) above 100, because it now pays more (d) about 97

**Q12.** A bond has one payment left: 105, due in exactly 1 year. At a 5% yield it costs 100. The yield jumps to 10%. What is its price now, and how much cash will it pay you in a year?
