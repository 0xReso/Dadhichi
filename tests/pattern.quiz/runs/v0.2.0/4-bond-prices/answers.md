# Answer key

## Mechanisms and the questions that test them

1. A bond's price is the value today of each future payment, discounted at the market yield. Q1, Q14, Q34, Q39, Q44, Q46
2. An existing bond's payments are fixed when it is issued; a rate change alters only its price. Q3, Q27, Q58
3. Yields and prices move in opposite directions. Q2, Q27, Q29, Q48
4. Coupon above the yield means a price above 100 (premium); equal means 100 (par); below means under 100 (discount). Q4, Q16, Q36, Q39, Q50
5. Current yield (coupon / price) is not the yield to maturity, because it ignores the gain or loss on the way to 100. Q5, Q20, Q40
6. Pull to par: with yields unchanged, a premium bond's price drifts down to 100 and a discount bond's drifts up. Q6, Q22, Q50, Q54
7. Held to maturity, a bond pays exactly what it promised; a price drop from a rate rise has disappeared by the end. Q12, Q35, Q45, Q53, Q59
8. The longer the maturity, the more the price moves for a given change in yield. Q7, Q23, Q42, Q43, Q56, Q57, Q60
9. The lower the coupon, the more the price moves for a given change in yield. Q8, Q28, Q60
10. Duration is the value-weighted average time until the bond's cash arrives; for a zero it equals maturity. Q9, Q24, Q42, Q43, Q49, Q59
11. Percentage price change ≈ −modified duration × change in yield, where modified duration = Macaulay duration / (1 + yield). Q13, Q25, Q41, Q52, Q55
12. Convexity: a fall in yield raises the price more than an equal rise lowers it, so the duration estimate overstates losses and understates gains. Q15, Q30, Q46
13. The same 1-point move hurts less when yields are already high. Q18, Q32, Q47, Q51
14. A perpetuity's price is coupon / yield, which makes it very sensitive. Q10, Q26, Q47, Q55
15. A floating-rate note's coupon resets to the market rate, so on a reset date it is worth 100. Q11, Q37, Q44, Q58, Q60
16. What moves a bond is its own yield: the government yield for its maturity plus its credit spread, and different maturities can move differently. Q17, Q31, Q48, Q51, Q57, Q58
17. Return over a holding period is coupons received plus the change in price. Q19, Q33, Q45, Q50, Q56
18. Coupons must be reinvested at future rates, so rising rates help a long holder's final total and falling rates hurt it. Q21, Q38, Q49, Q53

**Q1**
Why: 1. Discounting means dividing a future payment by (1 + yield) once for each year you wait, because money today could earn that yield. 2. Two years at 5%: 1.05 × 1.05 = 1.1025. 3. 100 / 1.1025 = 90.70.
Answer: (b) 90.70
The trap: (a), from subtracting 5 for each year as if interest were taken off the face value; (c) 90.91 uses simple interest, 100 / 1.10, ignoring that interest compounds; (d) treats the face value as today's price.
Rule: a payment's value today is the payment divided by (1 + yield) raised to the number of years until it arrives.

**Q2**
Why: 1. The bond's payments are fixed: 4, 4, then 104. 2. New buyers can now get 6% on new bonds, so they will only buy yours at a price that also gives them 6%. 3. Discounting at 6%: 4 / 1.06 + 4 / 1.1236 + 104 / 1.1910 = 3.77 + 3.56 + 87.32 = 94.65. 4. A higher discount rate shrinks every payment's value today.
Answer: (c) It falls below 100, to about 94.65.
The trap: (a), from the savings-account belief that higher rates are good for anyone who lends. That is true for new lending; your bond's rate was locked in. (b) confuses the final repayment with today's price; (d) assumes the coupon resets.
Rule: when yields rise, the price of an existing bond falls.

**Q3**
Why: 1. The savings account's rate follows the market, so 100 earns 6 next year. 2. The bond's coupon was written into it when it was issued: 4 per 100 of face value every year, whatever happens to rates afterwards. 3. The rate rise changes what the bond is worth to a buyer (its price), not what it pays you.
Answer: Savings account 6; bond 4.
The trap: answering 6 for both, from believing a bond's interest adjusts like a bank deposit's. Only floating-rate bonds do that.
Rule: a fixed-rate bond's payments never change after issue; when rates move, its price moves instead.

**Q4**
Why: 1. At a 6% yield, a bond paying 6 gives buyers exactly 6% at 100, so B costs 100. 2. C pays 2 a year more than the market requires, so buyers pay extra: 108.42. 3. A pays 2 a year less, so buyers pay less: 91.58. The 8.42 discount is the value today of the five missing 2s at 6%.
Answer: C (108.42), B (100.00, exactly 100), A (91.58).
The trap: putting A first or saying all three cost 100, from believing the price depends on the face value rather than on how the coupon compares with the yield.
Rule: a coupon above the yield means a premium, equal means par, below means a discount.

**Q5**
Why: 1. If held to the end, you collect 6 a year but get back only 100 for the 120 you paid, a loss of 20 spread over 5 years. 2. Current yield counts the coupon and ignores that loss. 3. Yield to maturity counts both, so it must be lower. 4. Solving 120 = the coupons and 100 discounted at y gives y ≈ 1.78%.
Answer: (c) below 5%, about 1.78%.
The trap: (a), from treating current yield as the return you earn. (d) treats the coupon rate as the return, ignoring the price paid; (b) gets the direction of the built-in loss backwards.
Rule: for a bond above 100, yield to maturity is below current yield, because the premium is lost by maturity.

**Q6**
Why: 1. A year from now the bond has 3 years left: payments of 8, 8 and 108. 2. Discounting at 5%: 7.62 + 7.26 + 93.29 = 108.17. 3. The extra 3 a year above the market rate is now owed for one year fewer, so it is worth less. 4. The price moves from 110.64 to 108.17, toward 100.
Answer: (c) below 110.64 but above 100, about 108.17.
The trap: (a), from believing a bond's price changes only when rates change. (d) jumps straight to the repayment value; (b) gets the drift backwards.
Rule: with yields unchanged, a premium bond's price drifts down toward 100 as maturity approaches.

**Q7**
Why: 1. A at 5%: 4 / 1.05 + 104 / 1.1025 = 98.14, a loss of 1.86%. 2. B at 5%: twenty 4s and 100, each discounted at 5% for up to 20 years, = 87.54, a loss of 12.46%. 3. The 1-point higher rate is applied once for each year a payment waits, so payments 20 years away are hit far harder than payments 1 or 2 years away.
Answer: (b) Bond B.
The trap: (c), from believing that the same change in yield means the same change in price. (d) confuses the final repayment with today's price.
Rule: the longer a bond has left, the more its price moves when its yield moves.

**Q8**
Why: 1. A at 5% is 76.83; at 6% it is 70.56, a loss of 8.17%. 2. B at 5% is 130.89; at 6% it is 122.08, a loss of 6.73%. 3. B receives much more of its value early, through its large coupons, and early cash is discounted for fewer years, so it is less affected. 4. A's value sits mostly in the repayment at year 10.
Answer: (a) A loses 8.2%, B loses 6.7%.
The trap: (b), from thinking the bond that pays more has more exposed to rates. (c) assumes the same maturity means the same risk; (d) multiplies the 1-point move by 10 years.
Rule: with the same maturity, the lower-coupon bond's price moves more.

**Q9**
Why: 1. Present values at 10%: 10 / 1.1 = 9.09 at year 1; 10 / 1.21 = 8.26 at year 2; 110 / 1.331 = 82.64 at year 3. They add up to the price, 100. 2. Weight each time by its share: (1 × 9.09 + 2 × 8.26 + 3 × 82.64) / 100 = (9.09 + 16.53 + 247.93) / 100 = 2.74.
Answer: (b) 2.74 years.
The trap: (a), from believing duration equals maturity, which is true only for a zero. (c) averages the payment times 1, 2, 3 equally; (d) weights by the cash amounts without discounting them first, (10 + 20 + 330) / 130 = 2.77.
Rule: duration weights each payment's time by its value today, so coupons pull it below maturity.

**Q10**
Why: 1. A perpetuity's price is coupon / yield, since a price P earning y a year forever produces exactly the coupon: P × y = 5. 2. At 5%: 5 / 0.05 = 100. 3. At 4%: 5 / 0.04 = 125. 4. The rise is 25%, because the payments stretch out forever and the far-off ones gain the most.
Answer: (d) 125
The trap: (c), from the straight-line duration estimate (a 1-point fall × duration 20 = +20%), which misses that the price curve bends upward. (a) assumes a bond that never repays does not react; (b) treats it like a 1-year bond.
Rule: a perpetuity is worth coupon / yield, so its price moves in proportion to 1 / yield.

**Q11**
Why: 1. From now on the FRN pays the market rate each year, and on each reset it gets the new market rate. 2. A bond that always pays exactly the yield buyers demand is worth 100, like a fixed bond whose coupon equals its yield. 3. At this moment its coupon (6%) matches the market (6%), so its price is 100. 4. The fixed bond is stuck paying 3 for 5 years, which is why it fell.
Answer: (a) 100
The trap: (b), from believing every 5-year bond moves the same way when rates rise. (c) assumes the higher coupon is a bonus, when it only matches the market; (d) guesses at a partial loss.
Rule: on a reset date an FRN's coupon equals the market rate, so its price is 100.

**Q12**
Why: 1. The only payment is 105 in one year. 2. At 10%, its value today is 105 / 1.10 = 95.45, down 4.55%. 3. The payment itself is fixed and the borrower pays as promised, so in a year you receive 105 whatever the market yield is then.
Answer: Price 95.45; cash in a year 105.
The trap: believing that the drop to 95.45 also cuts the cash you will receive, as if a price loss were money lost from the bond's payments.
Rule: a yield change moves the price, not the promised cash, so a holder who waits for maturity receives exactly what was promised.

**Q13**
Why: 1. Modified duration 7 means about 7% of the price per 1-point move in yield. (It equals Macaulay duration divided by (1 + yield).) 2. The move is 0.5 points, so the estimate is 7 × 0.5 = 3.5%. 3. Yields rose, so the price falls: 100 × (1 − 0.035) = 96.50.
Answer: (b) about 96.50
The trap: (a), from applying the full 7% regardless of the size of the move. (c) multiplies by 0.05 instead of 0.5, treating half a point as a twentieth of one; (d) gets the direction wrong.
Rule: percentage price change ≈ −modified duration × change in yield, in percentage points.

**Q14**
Why: 1. The buyer wants 6%, so discount your bond's payments at 6%. 2. Payments are 3 at year 1 and 103 at year 2. 3. 3 / 1.06 = 2.83 and 103 / 1.1236 = 91.67. 4. Total: 94.50. At that price, the 3 a year plus the gain to 100 gives exactly 6% a year.
Answer: (b) 94.50
The trap: (a), from subtracting the missing 3 a year for 2 years without discounting it (100 − 6). Shortfalls that arrive later are worth less today. (c) counts only one year's shortfall; (d) assumes the bond still sells at face value.
Rule: an old bond's price is whatever makes its fixed payments earn today's market yield.

**Q15**
Why: 1. At 5%: 100 / 1.05^20 = 37.69. 2. At 4%: 100 / 1.04^20 = 45.64, a gain of 21.1%. 3. At 6%: 100 / 1.06^20 = 31.18, a loss of 17.3%. 4. The price curve bends: as yields fall, each further fall moves the price more, and as yields rise, each further rise moves it less. This bend is called convexity.
Answer: The gain (+21.1%) is larger than the loss (−17.3%).
The trap: saying they are equal, from believing the price moves the same amount in either direction for the same size of yield move.
Rule: for an ordinary bond, an equal fall in yield helps more than an equal rise hurts.

**Q16**
Why: 1. At a 3% yield, a bond paying 3 would cost exactly 100. 2. This one costs 108, so buyers are accepting a lower return. 3. They also lose 8 between now and repayment, so the yield to maturity is below even the current yield, 3 / 108 = 2.78%. 4. Higher price, same payments, lower yield.
Answer: (c) below 3%
The trap: (b), from believing a more expensive bond must be paying a higher return. Paying more for the same payments earns less. (a) assumes coupon rate always equals yield; (d) confuses the repayment with the price.
Rule: a bond priced above 100 yields less than its coupon rate.

**Q17**
Why: 1. The company bond's yield is the government yield plus its spread: 3.5% + 2% = 5.5%, up from 5%. 2. Its payments, 5 a year and 100 at year 5, are fixed. 3. Discounting at 5.5% gives 97.86. 4. The government yield fell, but the company's own yield rose, and only its own yield sets its price.
Answer: about 97.9 (97.86)
The trap: answering above 100, from believing that when "interest rates" fall, every bond's price rises. Credit worries can push a company's yield the other way.
Rule: a bond's price follows its own yield, which is the benchmark yield plus its credit spread.

**Q18**
Why: 1. A zero's price is 100 / (1 + y)^10, so the percentage change is (1 + old y)^10 / (1 + new y)^10 − 1. 2. World X: (1.02 / 1.03)^10 − 1 = −9.30%. 3. World Y: (1.10 / 1.11)^10 − 1 = −8.65%. 4. Each extra point is a smaller fraction of 1 + y when y is already large.
Answer: (a) World X
The trap: (c), from treating a 1-point move as having the same effect at any starting level. (d) brings in something outside the setup: the question fixes the size of the move.
Rule: the same 1-point rise in yield cuts a bond's price by a smaller percentage when yields are already high.

**Q19**
Why: 1. Right after the jump, the price is 97.33, but you are not selling yet. 2. A year later the bond has 2 years left: 5 / 1.06 + 105 / 1.1236 = 98.17. 3. Over the year you collect a coupon of 5. 4. Net result: 5 + 98.17 − 100 = +3.17.
Answer: (c) a gain of about 3.2
The trap: (a), from treating the immediate price drop as the year's result and forgetting the coupon and the drift back toward 100. (b) counts the sale price but forgets the coupon; (d) forgets the price change entirely.
Rule: a holding-period return is the coupons received plus the change in price, and a rate rise does not automatically make it negative.

**Q20**
Why: 1. You pay 90, receive 3 at year 1 and 103 at year 2. 2. Yield to maturity is the y that makes 3 / (1 + y) + 103 / (1 + y)² = 90. 3. Trying y = 8.66%: 2.76 + 87.24 = 90.00. 4. Most of the return comes from the rise from 90 to 100, which current yield ignores.
Answer: (d) about 8.7%
The trap: (b), from treating current yield as the full return. (a) treats the coupon rate as the return; (c) counts only the price gain, 10 over 2 years on 90, and leaves out the coupon.

**Q21**
Why: 1. At 6%: the year-1 coupon grows for 2 years, 6 × 1.06² = 6.74; the year-2 coupon grows 1 year, 6.36; plus 106 at year 3. Total 119.10. 2. At 8%: 6 × 1.08² = 7.00; 6 × 1.08 = 6.48; plus 106. **Q23**

**Q24**

**Q25**

**Q26**

**Q27**

**Q28**

**Q29**

**Q30**

**Q31**

**Q32**

**Q37**

**Q38**
