<domain>
Field notes for finance, to draw on where they apply.

Orientation. Most finance topics sit on one of three maps: the life of money over time, where a sum today and a sum later are compared through an interest rate; the trade-off between risk and return, where a higher expected reward is the price of accepting a wider spread of outcomes; or the plumbing of markets, where buyers, sellers, brokers, exchanges and clearing houses move assets and cash between them. Placing a topic on one of these maps early tells the reader where they are.

Common foundations. A small set of ideas sits under a large share of finance topics: interest and compounding, where interest earns interest; the time value of money, which says a payment received sooner is worth more than the same payment later; present value, the amount today that is worth the same as a future payment once interest is taken into account; yield, the return an investment gives as a yearly rate; price and value, which are not the same thing; risk as the spread of possible outcomes, not only the chance of loss; diversification, which lowers risk by holding things that do not all fall together; and liquidity, how quickly something can be turned into cash without lowering its price. When a topic rests on one of these, that idea is usually a foundation.

Pairs that are often confused: price and yield on a bond, which move in opposite directions; nominal and real returns, before and after inflation; interest rate and annual percentage rate (APR), where APR adds fees; return and risk-adjusted return; stock and bond, ownership and a loan; saving and investing; revenue and profit; cash flow and profit; a fund's expense ratio and its total cost; correlation and causation in market data. If the topic touches one side of a pair, the reader may already be holding the other one by mistake.

Models and reality. Many finance results come from a model with stated assumptions, such as rates staying flat, markets being liquid, or past behaviour continuing. A model's answer is only as good as those assumptions, and the reader is best served by knowing which ones carry the weight.

Jurisdiction. Tax rules, account types, investor protections and even common terms differ between countries. Claims that depend on a country's rules are best marked with the country, or kept general.

Scale. Rough figures worth carrying: a change of 0.01 percentage points is one basis point, the unit traders use for rates; long-run inflation targets in many economies sit around 2 percent a year; money compounding at 7 percent a year roughly doubles in ten years. These figures make percentages tangible and explain why small rate changes matter over long periods.

Change. Rates, prices, limits and product terms change daily or yearly. Any number tied to a date belongs with its date.

Advice. How a financial product works is the same for everyone; whether it suits a particular person depends on their goals, time horizon, tax position and what else they hold. Regulators in many countries treat a personal recommendation differently from general information.
</domain>
